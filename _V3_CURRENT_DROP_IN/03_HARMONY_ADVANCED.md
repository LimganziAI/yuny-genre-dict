## SECTION 1 — REHARMONIZATION (SIX CORE TECHNIQUES)

Reharmonization replaces the chords beneath an existing melody
with new chords that harmonize the same melody differently. Each
technique produces a distinct color and emotional shift.

### 1.1 Tritone Substitution

**Principle**: A dominant 7 chord may be replaced by another
dominant 7 chord whose root is a tritone away. Both chords share
the same critical tritone (the 3rd and b7 of one is the b7 and
3rd of the other).

**Tritone Pairs**:
- G7 ↔ Db7 (both contain B and F)
- A7 ↔ Eb7 (both contain C# and G)
- D7 ↔ Ab7 (both contain F# and C)
- E7 ↔ Bb7 (both contain G# and D)
- F7 ↔ B7 (both contain A and Eb)
- C7 ↔ F#7 (both contain E and Bb)

**Application**:
- Standard ii-V-I in C: Dm7 - G7 - Cmaj7
- Tritone sub: Dm7 - Db7 - Cmaj7
- Effect: Chromatic bass descent D-Db-C (smooth voice leading)
- Genres: Jazz, bossa nova, neo-soul, R&B, gospel

**Variations**:
- Sub on the V only (most common): Dm7 - Db7 - Cmaj7
- Sub on the ii: Abm7 - G7 - Cmaj7 (less common, more dissonant)
- Sub on both: Abm7 - Db7 - Cmaj7 (full tritone reharm)

**Voicing notes**: Tritone substitutes work best when the b7 and
3 of the original V are preserved enharmonically. The substitute's
#11 (which is the original V's root) often sounds best in upper
voicing for added color.

### 1.2 Secondary Dominant Insertion

**Principle**: Insert a V7 chord before any non-tonic diatonic
chord, temporarily tonicizing it. Notation: V7/X means "V7 of X."

**Standard secondary dominants in C major**:
| Target | Secondary Dominant | Resolution |
|--------|-------------------|------------|
| ii (Dm) | A7 | A7 → Dm |
| iii (Em) | B7 | B7 → Em |
| IV (F) | C7 | C7 → F |
| V (G) | D7 | D7 → G |
| vi (Am) | E7 | E7 → Am |

**Application example**:
- Original: C - Am - Dm - G7 - C
- With V/V: C - A7 - Dm - G7 - C (A7 is V/V/V or "double dom")
- With V/ii and V/V: C - A7 - Dm - D7 - G7 - C

**Effect**: Adds chromatic motion, increases jazz character,
creates micro-tension cycles within otherwise diatonic progressions.

**Secondary leading-tone chords (vii°/X)**:
A diminished 7 chord built on the leading tone of any target
chord, resolving up by half step.
- vii°/V in C: F#dim7 → G7
- vii°/vi in C: G#dim7 → Am
- Effect: Smoother than full secondary dominant; common in
  classical and traditional songwriting.

### 1.3 Modal Mixture (Borrowed Chords)

**Principle**: Borrow chords from the parallel mode (parallel
minor when in major, parallel major when in minor). The most
useful modal mixture borrows from parallel minor into major
contexts.

**Borrowed chords from C minor into C major**:
| Borrowed Chord | Notes | Function |
|----------------|-------|----------|
| iv (Fm) | F-Ab-C | Bittersweet pre-tonic |
| bIII (Eb) | Eb-G-Bb | Mediant flat color |
| bVI (Ab) | Ab-C-Eb | Submediant flat |
| bVII (Bb) | Bb-D-F | Subtonic, rock anthem |
| iiø (Dm7b5) | D-F-Ab-C | Half-dim ii |
| v (Gm) | G-Bb-D | Modal cool minor v |

**Most valuable single borrow**: iv (minor four)
- Progression I-IV-iv-I (C-F-Fm-C) is the bittersweet ballad
  signature of pop, K-ballad, classic film score
- Examples: Beatles "In My Life", Adele "Someone Like You",
  countless Yoon Jong-shin compositions

**Common modal mixture progressions**:
- I-bVII-IV-I (C-Bb-F-C): Mixolydian rock anthem
- I-bIII-bVI-bVII-I (C-Eb-Ab-Bb-C): Epic cinematic
- I-iv-I (C-Fm-C): Lyrical bittersweet
- vi-bVI-iv-I (Am-Ab-Fm-C): Mournful descent
- I-bVI-bVII-I (C-Ab-Bb-C): Heroic, Star Wars-like

**Modal mixture in chorus turnarounds**: Replacing the predictable
IV chord with iv at the end of a phrase is the single most
effective bittersweet harmonic device in popular music.

### 1.4 Chromatic Mediant Substitution

**Principle**: Replace an expected diatonic chord with a chord
whose root is a major or minor 3rd away, retaining or shifting
the chord quality. Chromatic mediants share one common tone but
diverge in the other tones, producing a "magical shift" effect.

**Chromatic mediant pairs (in C major)**:
| Original | Chromatic Mediant | Common Tone |
|----------|-------------------|-------------|
| C major | E major (M3 up, both major) | E |
| C major | Eb major (m3 up, both major) | C |
| C major | Ab major (m3 down, both major) | C |
| C major | A major (M3 down, both major) | A |
| C major | E minor (M3 up, mode shift) | E and G |
| C major | Eb minor (m3 up, mode shift) | none |

**Effect**: Bypasses functional harmony for cinematic surprise.
Listener expects functional motion (V→I, IV→I); chromatic
mediant breaks expectation.

**Application examples**:
- "Creep" (Radiohead): G → B (chromatic mediant up M3, both major)
- "My Heart Will Go On": chromatic mediant in pre-chorus
- "Royals" (Lorde): chromatic context with mediant relations
- BTS "Spring Day": multiple chromatic mediant shifts in bridge
- Hans Zimmer film scores: signature chromatic mediant device

**Application conditions**:
- Use sparingly (one or two per song maximum)
- Pair with a deliberate emotional moment in lyric or arrangement
- Best applied at section boundaries (bridge entry, pre-chorus
  shift, key change)
- Avoid combining multiple chromatic mediants in succession
  (becomes harmonically incoherent)

### 1.5 Backdoor Cadence

**Principle**: Substitute V7 → I with iv7 → bVII7 → I. The bVII7
"sneaks in the back door" to tonic, bypassing the standard V7.

**Example in C major**:
- Standard: G7 → Cmaj7
- Backdoor: Fm7 → Bb7 → Cmaj7
- Bb7's b7 (Ab) resolves down to G (5 of C), and Bb7's 3 (D)
  resolves up to E (3 of C)

**Effect**: Soul, gospel, late-romantic flavor. Sounds inevitable
yet surprising. Often used in jazz turnarounds and gospel cadences.

**Variations**:
- Pure backdoor: Fm7 - Bb7 - Cmaj7
- Backdoor with ii-V setup: Dm7 - G7 - Fm7 - Bb7 - Cmaj7
  (the backdoor delays the expected resolution)
- Modal backdoor in minor: bVII7 → i (e.g., Bb7 → Cm)

### 1.6 Coltrane Changes (Major-Third Cycle)

**Principle**: Modulate by major 3rds, creating a three-key
cycle that returns to origin. Each new key is a major 3rd from
the previous, dividing the octave into three equal parts.

**Three-key cycles** (root motion divides octave evenly):
- C → E → Ab → C (major 3rd ascending)
- C → Ab → E → C (major 3rd descending)

**Standard Coltrane progression** (Giant Steps opening):
| Bmaj7 D7 | Gmaj7 Bb7 | Ebmaj7 | Am7 D7 | | Gmaj7 Bb7 | Ebmaj7 F#7 | Bmaj7 | Fm7 Bb7 |


The progression cycles through B, G, Eb keys in 3-bar units,
each linked by a V7 of the next key.

**Application beyond Giant Steps**:
- Reharmonize a static chord: replace 4 bars of Cmaj7 with
  Cmaj7 - Eb7 - Abmaj7 - B7 - Emaj7 - G7 - Cmaj7
- Use in bridge of a pop ballad to create harmonic intensity
- Apply to one section as a bridge device, return to original
  key for final chorus

**Why it works**: The major-3rd cycle creates a sense of
infinite ascent or descent that resolves on the third arrival,
producing both surprise and inevitability.

### 1.7 Counterpoint Reharmonization

**Principle**: Preserve the melody note as a chord tone (root,
3rd, 5th, 7th, 9th, etc.) while choosing harmonically distant
chords beneath it.

**Example**: Melody note C
| Chord | C functions as |
|-------|----------------|
| Cmaj7 | Root |
| Am7 | Minor 3rd (b3) |
| Fmaj7 | Major 5th |
| Dm7 | Minor 7th (b7) |
| Abmaj7#11 | Major 3rd |
| F#m7b5 | Tritone (avoid—dissonance, but possible) |

**Application**: Use this approach to reharmonize an existing
melody dramatically. Each chord change recolors the melody
without altering it.

## SECTION 2 — EXTENDED MODAL INTERCHANGE

Beyond simple borrowing from parallel minor, advanced modal
interchange draws from multiple parallel modes systematically.

### 2.1 Sources Available for Borrowing (in C major)

The seven parallel modes of C provide a rich palette:

| Mode | Notes | Distinctive Borrow Targets |
|------|-------|----------------------------|
| C Lydian | C D E F# G A B | II (D major) |
| C Ionian | C D E F G A B | (home) |
| C Mixolydian | C D E F G A Bb | bVII (Bb major) |
| C Dorian | C D Eb F G A Bb | iv (Fm), bIII (Eb) |
| C Aeolian | C D Eb F G Ab Bb | iv, bIII, bVI, bVII |
| C Phrygian | C Db Eb F G Ab Bb | bII (Db) |
| C Locrian | C Db Eb F Gb Ab Bb | bV (Gb dim) |

### 2.2 Modal Interchange Cascade

A bridge or pre-chorus may pass through multiple modal borrows
in sequence:

**Example cascade in C major**:
| F (IV) | Fm (iv from minor) | | Db (bII from Phrygian) | Bb (bVII from Mixolydian) | | C (back to I) | |


Effect: Each modal borrow injects a different emotional flavor.
Phrygian = exotic/dark, Lydian = bright/dreamy, Mixolydian =
bluesy/grounded, Aeolian = melancholy. The cascade creates a
sophisticated emotional journey within a single section.

### 2.3 Modal Mixture in Minor Keys

In minor keys, borrow from parallel major to create unexpected
brightness:

**Borrowed chords from C major into C minor**:
- I (C major): Picardy Third — final chord changes from minor
  to major, classic Renaissance/Baroque device
- IV (F major): brighter than diatonic iv
- bIII becomes III (Eb→E): dramatic reframing
- bVI becomes VI (Ab→A): unexpected major lift

## SECTION 3 — SPECIAL CHROMATIC CHORDS

### 3.1 Neapolitan Sixth (bII6)

**Principle**: A major triad built on the lowered 2nd scale
degree, typically appearing in first inversion (bII6).

**In C major**:
- bII = Db major (Db-F-Ab)
- bII6 = first inversion (F-Ab-Db, with F in bass)
- Function: Pre-dominant substitute, replacing IV or ii

**Resolution**:
- bII6 → V (most common): Db/F → G7
- bII6 → vii°7/V → V (with chromatic intermediary)

**Effect**: Dramatic minor-key tension, classical drama, surprise.
Famously used in "Don't Cry For Me Argentina" verse to chorus
transition, and throughout late-romantic classical music.

**In minor key context**:
The Neapolitan in minor (bII in C minor = Db major) is even
more dramatic, creating a stark contrast against the minor
tonic.

### 3.2 Augmented Sixth Chords

A family of chromatic chords containing the interval of an
augmented sixth (enharmonic minor 7th), functioning as terminal
pre-dominants. They resolve outward to the dominant.

**The augmented sixth interval in C major**: Ab to F# (the b6
to #4 of the key, both resolving outward to G).

**Three types**:

**Italian augmented sixth (It+6)**: 3 notes
- Notes: b6, 1, #4 (in C: Ab-C-F#)
- Resolution: Ab→G, F#→G (outward to dominant)

**French augmented sixth (Fr+6)**: 4 notes
- Notes: b6, 1, 2, #4 (in C: Ab-C-D-F#)
- Resolution: same outward motion plus D moves down to G or up
  to its octave

**German augmented sixth (Gr+6)**: 4 notes
- Notes: b6, 1, b3, #4 (in C: Ab-C-Eb-F#)
- Enharmonic equivalent: Ab7 (the same notes as Ab dominant 7)
- This enharmonic identity enables enharmonic modulation: a Gr+6
  in one key may be reinterpreted as Ab7 (V7 of Db)

**Application**: Augmented sixth chords are most common in
classical and film score. In pop/rock, they appear occasionally
in sophisticated bridge moments. They produce strong dramatic
push toward dominant resolution.

### 3.3 Common-Tone Diminished Seventh

**Principle**: A diminished 7 chord that shares one or more
tones with its target chord, used as a chromatic embellishment.

**Example in C major**:
- C#dim7 (C#-E-G-Bb) shares E and G with Cmaj7
- Used as approach: C#dim7 → Cmaj7 (chromatic neighbor approach)

**Application**: Adds tension and chromatic color while
ultimately resolving to the same tonic chord. Common in jazz
ballad arrangements and sophisticated pop.

### 3.4 Common-Tone Augmented Triad

**Principle**: An augmented triad sharing tones with diatonic
chords, used as transitional color.

**Example in C major**:
- Caug (C-E-G#) leads to F (G# resolves up to A, the 3rd of F)
- Effect: Voice-leading-driven tonal expansion

## SECTION 4 — CHROMATIC BASS LINES

Chromatic motion in the bass creates a sophisticated harmonic
foundation that drives the entire arrangement.

### 4.1 Descending Chromatic Bass

**Pattern in C major**:
Fmaj7 → E7 → Ebdim7 → Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7 Bass: F → E → Eb → D → G → C


The bass descends by half step from F to D, then jumps up to G
for the cadential dominant. Each chord harmonizes the next bass
note with appropriate chord quality.

**Application**: Bossa nova ballad, jazz ballad, neo-soul lush
sequences, sophisticated pop arrangements.

**Variation — full descent to tonic**:
Cmaj7 → Cmaj7/B → C7/Bb → F/A → Fm/Ab → C/G → ... Bass: C → B → Bb → A → Ab → G


### 4.2 Ascending Chromatic Bass

**Pattern in C major**:
C → C#dim → Dm7 → D#dim → Em7 → E7 → Fmaj7 Bass: C → C# → D → D# → E → E → F


Used in jazz and sophisticated pop for upward motion with
chromatic intermediaries between diatonic chord changes.

### 4.3 Pedal Points

**Tonic pedal**: Hold tonic note in bass while harmonies move
above.
- Bass: C held throughout
- Upper harmony: C - F - G - C
- Effect: Stability, drone, ambient anchor

**Dominant pedal**: Hold dominant note in bass for tension build.
- Bass: G held throughout (in C major)
- Upper harmony: D7 - G7 - D7 - G7
- Effect: Pre-cadential tension, build-up to drop

**Inverted pedal**: Hold a note in an upper voice while bass moves.
- Common in modal music and ambient production
- Creates drone-like upper-frequency anchor

## SECTION 5 — NON-FUNCTIONAL HARMONY

Contemporary indie, alternative, dream pop, and modern jazz often
employ harmony that does not follow functional V→I logic. The
chords work through other organizing principles.

### 5.1 Common Tone Voice-Leading Driven

Chords are chosen for smooth voice leading and shared tones
rather than functional relationships.

**Example progression in C major sphere**:

Cmaj7 → Em7 → Am7 → Fmaj7 → Cmaj7 Common tones: E throughout, smooth descent in inner voices


The progression has no V chord, no functional cadence, yet works
because each chord transition preserves multiple common tones
and produces stepwise voice motion.

### 5.2 Color-Driven Progressions

Chords are selected for their atmospheric color rather than
their functional role.

**Example (Beach House style)**:
Cmaj7 → G/B → Am7 → Fmaj7 (loop indefinitely)


The progression cycles without seeking resolution. The Cmaj7's
shimmer, Am7's melancholy, and Fmaj7's openness create a mood
without harmonic narrative.

### 5.3 Pedal-Anchored Harmony

A bass pedal allows upper voices to move through unrelated chords
without losing tonal anchor.

**Example with C bass pedal**:
C/C → D/C → Eb/C → F/C → G/C Upper: stepwise motion D, Eb, F, G against fixed bass


Each chord is harmonically distinct but the C bass anchors
everything. Used extensively in modern film score and ambient.

### 5.4 Modal Vamps

Two- or three-chord vamps within a single mode, creating mood
without progression.

**Dorian vamp examples**:
- Cm7 - F (i-IV in C Dorian): "So What" by Miles Davis
- Dm - G (i-IV in D Dorian): countless modal jazz tunes

**Lydian vamp examples**:
- Fmaj7 - G/F (I-II/I in F Lydian): floating, dreamy
- C - D/C (I-II/I in C Lydian): film score signature

**Mixolydian vamp**:
- G - F (I-bVII in G Mixolydian): rock and Celtic folk
- C - Bb (I-bVII in C Mixolydian): Beatles "She Said She Said"

### 5.5 Frank Ocean Style Modal-Diatonic Hybrid

Frank Ocean's "Pink + White" demonstrates non-functional indie
R&B harmony. The song's verse uses Eb major / C minor harmony
that floats between modes:
Ebmaj7 / Cm7 / Fm7 / Bb7sus4


The Bb7sus4 never resolves to Eb in the expected way; instead
the progression loops, treating harmony as texture rather than
grammar.

### 5.6 Indie / Dream Pop Conventions

Contemporary indie often uses:
- Sus chords (sus2, sus4) instead of clean triads
- Add9 colors throughout (no clean triads at all)
- Open-string drones on guitar (often DADGAD or open-G tunings)
- Stepwise descending bass under static melody (Pachelbel-cousin)
- Pedal tones over shifting upper harmony
- Avoidance of clean V7→I cadences (too "pop")

**Example indie progression** (Big Thief style):
Dadd9 → A/C# → Bm11 → G6/9 Voicing: open strings drone D and A, upper voices shift

## SECTION 6 — MODULATION TECHNIQUES

Modulation is the change of tonal center within a piece. Different
modulation types produce different effects.

### 6.1 Pivot Chord Modulation

**Principle**: Find a chord that exists in both the old and new
keys, treat it as a pivot.

**Example: C major → G major**:
- Am is vi in C, ii in G — natural pivot
- Progression: C → F → Am (pivot) → D7 → G

**Use**: Smooth, classical, related keys. Most common modulation
technique in traditional Western music.

**Common pivot chords for adjacent keys (sharp side)**:
| From | To | Common Pivot Chords |
|------|-----|-----|
| C major | G major | Am (vi/ii), Em (iii/vi), C (I/IV) |
| C major | D major | Em (iii/ii), Am (vi/v—altered) |
| C major | F major | C (I/V), Am (vi/iii), Dm (ii/vi) |

### 6.2 Direct (Truck Driver) Modulation

**Principle**: Abrupt key change at section boundary, no pivot.

**K-pop standard**: Half-step or whole-step lift before final
chorus. From E major up to F major; from G major up to A major.

**Application**:
- End bridge or interlude with V7 of new key for one bar
- Drop into new key on final chorus downbeat
- Combine with crash cymbal, layered harmony entry, vocal
  ad-lib for maximum impact

**Examples**:
- Whitney Houston "I Will Always Love You": E major → F major
- Beyoncé "Love On Top": modulates 4 times, each up a half step
- BTS "Dynamite": multiple modulations
- Most K-pop hits 2010s onward: at least one final-chorus lift

### 6.3 Common-Tone Modulation

**Principle**: Hold one note while re-spelling the chords around
it, transitioning to a distant key.

**Example C major → E major**:
- C chord (C-E-G) sustains
- E note held, becomes the root of new key
- Transition to E major (E-G#-B)
- Effect: Magical, distant, cinematic

**Use**: Film score moments of revelation, romantic-era classical.

### 6.4 Chromatic Modulation

**Principle**: Use a chromatic chord (often diminished 7 or
German augmented 6th) as enharmonic pivot to a distant key.

**Example using diminished 7**:
- C°7 = Eb°7 = F#°7 = A°7 (enharmonically identical)
- In C major, C°7 might function as vii°/i in C
- Reinterpret as F#°7 = vii°/V in C minor → modulates abruptly

**Use**: Romantic-era classical, advanced jazz, dramatic film
score moments.

### 6.5 Sequential Modulation

**Principle**: Repeat a phrase, transposed stepwise, until
reaching new key.

**Example**:
- Phrase 1 in C major (4 bars)
- Same phrase up a whole step in D major (4 bars)
- Same phrase up a whole step in E major (4 bars)
- Effect: Cinematic build-up, tension escalation

**Use**: Climactic film score sequences, prog rock, classical
development sections.

### 6.6 Modal Modulation

**Principle**: Treat current tonic as a different scale degree
in destination key.

**Example C major → A minor**:
- C is I in C major, also III in A minor
- Reframe C as III chord, drop to A minor tonic
- This is the relative minor shift, but conceptualized as modal
  pivot rather than relative key swap

## SECTION 7 — EXPERIMENTAL HARMONIC DEVICES

### 7.1 Negative Harmony

**Principle**: Mirror the diatonic system around a central axis
(typically the line between b3 and 5 of the key, or between 4
and 5). Each chord has a "negative" counterpart.

**Concept origin**: Ernst Levy (1968), popularized by Jacob
Collier in 2010s.

**In C major, mirror axis between Eb and G**:
- C major (I) ↔ C minor (i)
- F major (IV) ↔ G minor (vminor)
- G major (V) ↔ F minor (iv)
- Am (vi) ↔ Eb major (bIII)

**Application**: Reharmonize a progression using its negative
counterpart for each chord. The result is harmonically distinct
but emotionally related.

**Standard ii-V-I in negative**:
- Standard: Dm7 - G7 - Cmaj7
- Negative: Cm7 - Fm7 - Cmaj7 (actually a minor cadence shifted)

Negative harmony rarely replaces an entire piece; it more often
provides reharmonization options or single-chord substitutes.

### 7.2 Polychords

**Principle**: Stack two chords from different harmonic regions
to produce composite harmony.

**Notation**: Upper chord / Lower chord
- D/C: D major triad over C bass and possibly C major lower
  voicing
- F#m/C: F# minor over C major (creates rich tension)

**Application**: Modern jazz comping, modern film score, post-bop,
experimental rock. Polychords occupy a sonic space between single
chord and full bitonal harmony.

### 7.3 Slash Chord Harmony as Function

Modern producers use slash chords not as inversions but as
intentional non-functional colors:

- F/G: F triad over G bass (sus chord substitute, common in
  modern pop pre-chorus)
- C/E in modal context: not a true 1st inversion, but a modal
  shimmer
- Am7/G: Am7 with G in bass, ambiguous between Am7 and Cmaj7/G

These voicings escape the I-IV-V grid and produce modal,
floating harmony.

### 7.4 Quartal and Quintal Harmony

**Quartal**: Stack perfect 4ths (covered in Foundations §10.6)
**Quintal**: Stack perfect 5ths

**Quintal voicing example**: C-G-D-A-E
- All adjacent intervals are perfect 5ths
- Produces wide, open, ambiguous harmony
- Used in modern fusion, ambient, post-rock

### 7.5 Microtonal and Just Intonation Color

While most pop production stays in 12-tone equal temperament,
microtonal effects appear as production-driven color:

- Detuned vocal stacks (formant-shifted by ±10-25 cents)
- Pitch bend ornaments (especially in K-traditional fusion)
- Just-intonation chord voicings (Jacob Collier's signature
  vocal stacks use pure intonation tunings)

These devices appear in advanced production but rarely require
notation; they emerge from tuning and recording choices.

## SECTION 8 — APPLIED EXAMPLES (CONTEMPORARY ANALYSIS)

### 8.1 K-Pop Modulation Patterns

K-pop choruses commonly modulate up a half step or whole step
in the final chorus, sometimes paired with a chromatic mediant
into the bridge.

**Pattern example**:
- Verse and chorus 1-2 in E major
- Bridge in G major (chromatic mediant up m3, both major)
- Final chorus in F major (half step up from original E)
- Net effect: two distinct modulations creating a sense of
  ascending emotional journey

### 8.2 Frank Ocean / Modern R&B Non-Functional Harmony

Modern R&B often uses progressions that loop through diatonic
and modal chords without strong cadential motion:

**Pink + White verse pattern (simplified)**:
Ebmaj7 → Cm7 → Fm7 → Bb (loop)


The Bb at the end is a IV in E♭ major or a III in C minor —
ambiguous tonal center. The progression breathes rather than
cycles toward resolution.

### 8.3 Indie Folk Bittersweet Construction

Bon Iver, Phoebe Bridgers, and similar artists use modal
mixture and sus chord vocabulary extensively:

**Typical bittersweet verse**:
Dadd9 → A/C# → Bm11 → Gmaj7 → Em7 → Asus4 → A


The progression mostly diatonic in D major, with sus4 chords
and added 9ths providing the softness rather than chromatic
chords.

### 8.4 Film Score Cinematic Harmony (Hans Zimmer Style)

Film score uses chromatic mediants and pedal points for
mythic scale:

**Heroic theme construction**:
C pedal in bass throughout, upper: | Cmaj | E♭maj | Cmaj | F minor | | Cmaj | A♭maj | Bb maj | Cmaj


The C pedal anchors emotion while the upper voicings shift
through chromatic mediants and modal borrowed chords.

### 8.5 Jazz Standard Reharmonization (Coltrane Application)

Take a simple ii-V-I in C and apply Coltrane changes:

**Original**: Dm7 - G7 - Cmaj7

**Coltrane reharm**:
| Dm7 Eb7 | Abmaj7 B7 | Emaj7 G7 | Cmaj7 |


Each pair of chords cycles through one of the three Coltrane
tonal centers (B-G-Eb), arriving at C through the final ii-V.

## SECTION 9 — APPLICATION GUIDE

### 9.1 When to Use Advanced Techniques

**Use advanced harmony when**:
- The diatonic version sounds predictable to the production target
- A specific emotional moment needs heightened color (bridge climax,
  pre-chorus tension, final chorus payoff)
- The genre conventions reward harmonic sophistication (jazz,
  neo-soul, modern R&B, sophisticated indie, film score)
- A standard cadence would feel like cliché in the project context

**Avoid advanced harmony when**:
- Genre demands simplicity (punk, garage rock, traditional country,
  worship, EDM big-room)
- Vocalist or instrumentalist cannot navigate complex changes
- The melody is the primary content and complex harmony would
  obscure it
- Listener simulation suggests the audience would experience
  harmony as friction

### 9.2 Application Hierarchy

When considering reharmonization, apply techniques in this order
of impact-to-disruption ratio:

1. **Modal mixture iv borrowing** — single chord swap, maximum
   bittersweet effect with minimal disruption
2. **Secondary dominant insertion** — adds chromatic motion
   while preserving original cadence
3. **Tritone substitution** — replaces V7 with chromatic bass
   descent
4. **Chromatic mediant in bridge** — single dramatic shift,
   preserves rest of song
5. **Modal interchange cascade** — rich color but requires careful
   voice leading
6. **Backdoor cadence** — sophisticated alternative to V-I
7. **Coltrane changes** — requires extended harmonic sophistication
   throughout
8. **Negative harmony reharm** — full reframe, requires entire
   progression rebuild

### 9.3 Common Pitfalls

- **Stacking too many techniques**: Combining tritone sub,
  chromatic mediants, and Coltrane changes in one short song
  produces incoherence rather than sophistication
- **Ignoring melody compatibility**: Reharmonization must keep
  the melody as a chord tone or accepted tension on strong beats
- **Losing the form's emotional arc**: Advanced harmony in every
  section flattens the dynamic; reserve it for specific moments
- **Overusing the bittersweet iv**: Becomes predictable trick
  if every chorus uses I-V-vi-iv
- **Borrowing without voice leading**: Modal mixture works only
  when the borrowed chord voices smoothly into and out of
  surrounding chords
- **Modulation without preparation**: Direct modulation works in
  pop with strong arrangement support; in other contexts it
  needs at least a pivot

## SECTION 10 — MICROTONAL & XENHARMONIC HARMONY (NEW v2.2 / 2026-05)

화성 옵션의 새 차원. 평균율(12-TET) 바깥의 음을 의도적으로
사용하는 화성 시스템. 회의록(2026-05-08) 트리거로 정식 등록.

### 10.1 미분음(Microtone) 정의

**미분음**: 평균율 반음(100 cents)보다 작은 음정.
- 1/4 tone = 50 cents (24-EDO)
- 1/6 tone ≈ 33 cents
- 1/8 tone = 25 cents

**Xenharmonic**: 12-TET 바깥의 모든 튜닝 시스템.
포함하는 분야: 24-EDO, 19-EDO, 22-EDO, 31-EDO, Just Intonation
(JI), Pythagorean, mean-tone, 마이크로 디튠.

### 10.2 사용 목적 두 갈래

**(A) 곡 전체 미분음 (Whole-Track Xenharmonic)**:
- 24-EDO/22-EDO/19-EDO 같은 시스템 위에 곡 구축
- 동양·중동·실험·아방가르드 컨셉
- Suno 구현 난이도: ★★★★ (Suno는 평균율 편향, 완전 구현 어려움)
- 효과: 이질적·초현실적·의식적 분위기

**(B) 부분적 미분음 / 의도된 디튠 (Targeted Microtonal Color)**:
- 평균율 곡 안에서 특정 순간·악기·라인만 디튠
- 모든 장르에서 사용 가능
- Suno 구현 난이도: ★★ (검증된 어법 다수 존재)
- 효과: "꽂히게 만드는" 의도된 불협, 라이브 악기적 풍성함

회의록 핵심 통찰: 라이브 악기가 풍성하게 들리는 이유 =
정확하지 않은 튜닝. 전자악기·가상악기는 너무 정확해서
인공적으로 들림. 의도된 디튠이 organic 느낌을 만듦.

### 10.3 24-EDO (Quarter-Tone) 시스템

가장 흔한 미분음 시스템. 옥타브를 24개 균등 분할.
12개 표준 음 + 그 사이 12개 quarter-tone.

**표기법**:
- 반올림: half-sharp (♯ 위에 추가 표시)
- 반내림: half-flat (♭ 위에 추가 표시)
- 또는: C↑ (quarter-tone up), C↓ (quarter-tone down)

**활용 모드**:
- Maqam (아랍 전통): half-flat 2도, half-flat 6도 등
- Bohlen-Pierce 시스템 일부
- 현대 작곡 (Carrillo, Wyschnegradsky, Haba)

**대표 사용 작곡가/장르**:
- 클래식 현대: Charles Ives, Ben Johnston, Harry Partch
- 록/메탈: King Gizzard & The Lizard Wizard "Flying Microtonal Banana"
- 재즈: Joe Maneri (microtonal jazz pioneer)
- 영화음악: 이질감 표현용 (호러, 사이파이)

### 10.4 22-EDO 시스템

옥타브 22 균등 분할. 12-TET보다 7th 화음·블루스 표현이
풍부함. Paul Erlich가 정립한 시스템.

**특징**:
- "Pajara" temperament 구조
- 5도(7\22) 약간 넓음 → 7th 화음 조화로움
- 블루스 노트 자연스럽게 통합

**Suno 적용 가능성**:
부분 표현 가능. "22-EDO blues progression" 어법으로
실험 가치 있음.

### 10.5 19-EDO 시스템

옥타브 19 균등 분할. 미넌-톤(meantone) 계열에 적합.
르네상스·바로크 음악의 '순정율적' 색채 강화 가능.

### 10.6 Just Intonation (JI / 순정율)

수학적 비율로 정의된 음정. 5:4 = 장3도 (정확),
3:2 = 5도 (정확). 평균율의 "약간 어긋남" 없음.

Jacob Collier가 보컬 스택에서 자주 사용 — pure intonation
voicing이 평균율과 다른 색채를 만들어냄.

### 10.7 의도된 디튠 (Microtonal Detune) — 실용 어법

**용어 분류**:

| 효과 | Cent 범위 | 사용 위치 |
|------|----------|----------|
| Subtle organic | ±5-10 cents | 보컬 더블, 신스 패드 |
| Noticeable warm | ±10-25 cents | 신스 layer, 코러스 효과 |
| Dramatic tension | ±25-50 cents | 솔로 라인, 드론 |
| Quarter-tone | ±50 cents | 미분음 멜로디 |
| Full microtone | 변동 | xenharmonic 곡 |

**라이브 악기 디튠 시뮬레이션**:
- 기타 단일 노트 ±3-7 cents (자연스러운 손가락 압력 변화)
- 브라스 ±5-10 cents (호흡·립 텐션)
- 스트링 솔로 ±5-15 cents (비브라토 + 인토네이션)
- 보컬 ±5-20 cents (멜로디 표현)

**비-라이브 악기에 라이브 느낌 주기**:
- 신스 패드 detune ±10-15 cents (super-saw 본질)
- 신스 리드 vibrato 의도화 (LFO 5-7Hz, depth ±10 cents)
- 808 sub-bass slight pitch glide (drill bass 시그니처)

### 10.8 의도된 불협 (Intentional Dissonance) — 화성 차원

회의록 핵심: "더블 기타인데 각각 다른 코드로 연주해서
화성 조합 혹은 일부러 불협화음을 일으킨다."

이는 다음 기법들의 종합:

**(a) Bitonal Doubling**:
같은 멜로디를 두 다른 키로 동시 연주.
예: 기타 1 = C major, 기타 2 = Bb major (전체적으로 C dorian
색채). Bartók, Ives 사용.

**(b) Cluster Voicing**:
2도(반음/온음) 간격으로 음 쌓기.
예: C-D-Eb-F를 동시에. Penderecki, Crumb 사용.
모던 영화음악·앰비언트에서 텐션 표현.

**(c) Polychordal Layering**:
2개의 다른 트라이어드를 동시 연주.
예: 상단 D major + 하단 C major.
Stravinsky "Petrushka chord" (C + F#).

**(d) Quartal/Quintal Stacking with Friction**:
4도/5도 쌓되, 한 음에 마이크로 디튠 적용.
모던 재즈 (McCoy Tyner) + 실험 어법.

**(e) Detuned Unison Layering**:
같은 멜로디를 두 악기로 연주, 한쪽만 ±15 cents 디튠.
의도된 phasing. 1970s psychedelic, 모던 indie 어법.

### 12.1 정의

같은 멜로디 또는 다른 멜로디를 두 기타가 서로 다른 키 또는
모드로 동시 연주. 결과적으로 polychord 또는 bitonal harmony
생성.

### 12.2 Bitonal Doubling 패턴

**Pattern 1 — Same Melody, Different Keys**:
- 기타 1: C major scale 멜로디
- 기타 2: 같은 멜로디를 Bb major로 (장2도 아래)
- 결과: parallel motion 불협 + 풍성

**Pattern 2 — Different Chords, Locked Rhythm**:
- 기타 1: C major triad strumming
- 기타 2: F# major triad strumming (tritone away)
- 결과: maximum dissonance, 영화적 텐션 (Stravinsky pattern)

**Pattern 3 — Major + Minor Same Root**:
- 기타 1: C major chord
- 기타 2: C minor chord
- 결과: blues-color 강제 표현 (3rd-3rd 동시 울림)

**Pattern 4 — Harmonic Skin Layer**:
- 기타 1: 메인 코드 진행 (정상)
- 기타 2: 한 옥타브 위에서 4도 sus stacking
- 결과: 모던 jazz / post-rock 색채
